EBITDA Add-backs Buyers Accept — and Reject

Add-backs are where seller optimism meets buyer skepticism. In a light QoE review, every adjustment to reported EBITDA is tested against one question: can you prove it? Documentation — not narrative — decides what survives.

Adjustments that typically hold up

  • One-off, documented items: litigation settlements, restructuring charges, or asset write-offs with invoices and board notes.
  • Owner compensation normalization: market-rate salary substitution supported by comparables, not round numbers.
  • Related-party items: rent or salaries paid to group entities at non-market terms, clearly evidenced.

Adjustments buyers strike out

  • “Synergies” and projected savings — these belong in the business case, not historical EBITDA.
  • Recurring costs relabeled as discretionary without evidence of discontinuation.
  • Rounding errors compounding in the seller's favor — small unsupported add-backs erode credibility for the large ones.

Revenue quality matters too

A QoE isn't only about costs. Revenue recognition timing, cost allocations, and customer concentration all shape sustainable EBITDA. A customer that is 40% of revenue changes the multiple conversation before any add-back does.

Practical rule

If an add-back cannot be tied to a source document within minutes, treat it as unsupportable. Our FDD workstreams test each adjustment exactly that way — see Financial Due Diligence.

Frequently asked questions

What makes an add-back credible?

Three things: it is non-recurring, it is evidenced, and it is a genuine cost of running the business under current ownership that will not continue under new ownership. If any of those is missing, the buyer will challenge it.

What are the add-backs buyers reject most often?

Saved overhead that was never actually removed. Synergies that belong to the buyer, not the seller. Costs that recur every year but are labelled one-off. And related-party transactions that are not restated to arm's length.

Should a pack include negative adjustments?

Yes. A pack that only adjusts upward is not a quality of earnings exercise; it is a marketing document. Including the adjustments a buyer would make anyway — fair market rent, under-provisioned costs, discretionary savings that will not survive — makes the credible add-backs more believable.
